If Your AI Cannot Explain What It Did, You Cannot Properly Govern It

An engineering approach to accountability in autonomous systems.

Walker Pollitt LLC · November 27, 2026

Artificial intelligence has made it remarkably easy to produce answers. Establishing whether those answers are trustworthy remains an entirely different engineering problem.

Consider an AI system that reviews maintenance documentation and recommends replacing a piece of equipment.

Its recommendation may be correct. But what evidence did it use? Was that evidence current? Which requirements influenced its decision? Did it have permission to initiate a purchase or merely recommend one?

Those questions become increasingly important as AI moves from answering questions to performing work.

A confident answer is not evidence

A language model can generate an extremely convincing explanation without that explanation accurately representing how its output was produced.

Therefore, asking an AI to explain itself after completing a task is not a substitute for recording what actually occurred.

We need independently observable evidence.

In traditional engineering, we preserve commissioning records, test results, equipment identification, revision histories and acceptance criteria.

Intelligent automation deserves comparable discipline.

The minimum viable evidence trail

Small businesses do not necessarily need an expensive governance platform to begin implementing accountable automation.

Start by recording seven categories of information.

EvidencePurpose
Task identifierConnect related operations
UTC timestampsEstablish when events occurred
Model and configuration versionsIdentify the operating environment
Input referencesIdentify the evidence considered
Tool operations and resultsEstablish observable actions
Authorization decisionsDetermine what was permitted
Completion and validation statusEstablish whether the task succeeded

These records should come from the application, execution environment and authorization mechanisms wherever possible, rather than relying exclusively on the AI's description of its own behavior.

Separate recommendations from authority

Suppose an automated inspection system identifies an abnormal temperature reading.

It might recommend shutting down a piece of equipment.

That does not mean it should automatically possess the authority to do so.

The distinction between recommending an action and authorizing that action is fundamental to engineering safe systems.

An independent authorization mechanism should evaluate the applicable permissions, operating conditions and required approvals before allowing consequential actions.

Instrumentation is a practical starting point

OpenTelemetry provides a standardized approach to collecting application telemetry, including traces, metrics and logs.

Using technologies such as Azure Monitor Application Insights, developers can associate related operations through trace identifiers and introduce additional application-specific telemetry.

However, an ordinary application trace is not automatically a complete or tamper-resistant audit record. Sampling, retention limits, access permissions and incomplete instrumentation can all affect the available evidence.

Security-critical authorization records may require separate, appropriately protected storage.

Protect the information you collect

Recording everything is not responsible governance.

Credentials, proprietary customer information, sensitive infrastructure details and personally identifiable information should not be indiscriminately captured in diagnostic logs.

Government and defense contractors must also consider the specific information-handling requirements of their agreements before transferring telemetry to external services.

Collect what is necessary. Protect what is collected. Retain it only for an appropriate period.

Engineering the ability to learn from failure

The real value of a properly constructed evidence trail becomes apparent when something goes wrong.

Without reliable records, engineers are forced to reconstruct events through assumptions.

With reliable records, they can investigate failures, identify dependencies, reproduce conditions and improve the system.

Accountability is not something we should ask an intelligent system to provide after the fact. It is something we should engineer into the system from the beginning.
